## Claude

Good instinct on "inference" — and the same move works for almost every other term in that brief. The assignment is written in deliberately system-level vocabulary, which means it's inviting you to optimize a *pipeline*, not a kernel. Here's how each load-bearing word maps onto your verifier, and which ones you actually need to nail down before the framing is safe.

**"AI execution system."** Not a model — a *runtime* that orchestrates model calls, tools, and control flow to do work. This is the most permissive term in the brief. Your verifier is exactly this: it takes a problem PDF + candidate solution and runs a sequence of checks (parsing, dimensional analysis, numeric/symbolic verification, LLM adjudication) to emit a correctness judgment. "Execution" also implies inference-time, not training — so you're never on the hook for fine-tuning, which is the right scope for the time budget.

**"inference costs."** You've got this right — read it as the total cost of running the system to produce one output, i.e. **$ per problem-set verified** (tokens × price, summed across every model/tool call in the pipeline, not one forward pass). Making this interpretation *explicit* in the writeup is worth doing, because "how you define the problem" is something they say they're grading.

**"workload."** A bounded class of tasks the system handles. Yours is crisp: *verifying IPhO-mechanics-level problems and solutions for correctness.* The brief explicitly blesses focusing on "a particular workload," and verification is a genuinely good one to pick because its economics are asymmetric — checking is often far cheaper than generating, and many errors (dimensional, boundary-case, sign, order-of-magnitude) are cheaply detectable without a frontier model. That asymmetry is *where your 50% actually comes from*, so name it early.

**"application."** The end-use context — competition-prep problem generation. The verifier is one component of that; you're optimizing the component, not the whole app. Fine, but say so, so the boundary is clear.

**"model family" / "infrastructure layer" / "combination of techniques."** These three are the brief telling you which levers are on the table: routing across model sizes (cheap model for easy checks, frontier only when needed), serving-layer tricks (prompt caching, batching), and — crucially — *combining* them. A cascade + tool-use (symbolic solver / code execution) + prompt caching verifier is squarely inside what they're asking for.

Now the two terms that are load-bearing and where framings quietly go wrong:

**"reducing by 50%."** A percentage is meaningless without a baseline, and the credibility of your whole submission rests on that baseline being *fair* — what a competent engineer would actually ship (e.g. "frontier model reads problem + solution with a strong verification prompt, every time"), not a strawman you sandbagged so your number looks good. Evaluators reading these look hard for exactly this. Define the baseline first, measure its cost and quality, *then* show your optimized system halves the cost.

**"maintaining equal quality."** This is a *non-inferiority* constraint, not an improvement target — and it forces you to define what "quality" means for a verifier, which is your best "test your assumptions" moment. Raw accuracy is the wrong metric here: a verifier that passes a wrong solution (false negative) is far more damaging to the application than one that flags a correct solution for human review (false positive). So your quality metric should probably be **recall on catching genuine errors** (or a cost-weighted error rate), held equal to baseline, while cost drops. Calling that out shows judgment the accuracy-number crowd will miss.

Put together, an aligned one-line problem statement you could drop into the writeup:

> *Given the workload of verifying IPhO-level physics solutions, design an inference-time execution system that halves cost-per-verification versus a strong single-frontier-model baseline, while holding error-catching recall statistically non-inferior — via a cascade that spends cheap deterministic and small-model checks first and escalates to a frontier model only on genuinely ambiguous cases.*

That fits every term in the brief without stretching any of them.

One thing worth deciding before you build: do you scope strictly to *the verifier's own cost* (cleanest, most demonstrable in limited time), or do you go broader and argue the verifier *enables* cheaper generation (generate-with-cheap-model-then-verify beats one expensive generation)? The second is more ambitious but much harder to hold quality constant and to demonstrate credibly in the time. Which of those two are you leaning toward — I can pressure-test the baseline and eval design for whichever you pick.

## Claude

On the first phrase — "statistically non-inferior recall" just means: **the cheap system catches wrong solutions at least as reliably as the expensive baseline does.** "Recall" = of all the genuinely broken solutions, what fraction did the verifier flag. "Non-inferior" = not meaningfully worse — and "statistically" is only there to say a tiny gap that's within measurement noise doesn't count as worse. Plain version: *"while catching bad solutions just as reliably as the baseline."*

On the second point — you're right, and it's the more important catch. A problem statement that names the cascade has already smuggled the answer in, which weakens exactly the thing they're grading ("how you define the problem" as distinct from how you solve it). It also boxes you in: if mid-build the evidence says routing-by-difficulty beats a cascade, or that a symbolic-solver-first design wins, your own problem statement is now fighting you. Keep the statement about *what must be true of the system*; let the solution section own *how*.

Stripped-down version:

> *Given the workload of verifying IPhO-level physics solutions, design an inference-time execution system that cuts cost-per-verification by 50% versus a strong single-frontier-model baseline, while catching bad solutions at least as reliably as that baseline.*

Four things pinned, zero mechanism: the workload, the baseline to beat, the cost target, the quality floor. Everything about *how* — cascades, tool use, routing, caching, cheaper models, or some combination — stays in the solution space where you can let evidence pick the winner.

If you want to leave yourself even more room, you can generalize "single-frontier-model baseline" to "a strong baseline that a competent engineer would ship" — but I'd keep it concrete, because a vague baseline is the easiest place for a 50%-reduction claim to lose credibility. Naming it fixes the reference point you're measuring against.
